# **Qualitative Forecasting of Argentina's Political-Economic Evolution: Macroeconomic Seed-Data Curation and Simulation Framework**

## **Macroeconomic State of Argentina as of 31 January 2025**

As of the strict information cutoff date of 31 January 2025, the Argentine economy was positioned at a fragile but historic stabilization pivot following the first full year of President Javier Milei’s administration, defined by a severe fiscal contraction, a sharp deceleration in headline inflation, and the persistence of rigid foreign exchange controls. The national government successfully closed the 2024 fiscal year with its first annual financial surplus in 14 years, achieving a primary surplus of 1.8% of GDP and a financial surplus of 0.3% of GDP, an outcome driven by unprecedented real-term cuts to capital transfers, provincial funding, and pension expenditures.1 Simultaneously, headline consumer inflation decelerated to 2.7% month-over-month in December 2024 (accumulating 117.8% for the year), prompting the Central Bank (BCRA) to announce a strategic reduction in the official exchange rate’s crawling peg from 2% to 1% monthly, effective February 2025, in an attempt to anchor inflation expectations further.5 While this macroeconomic stabilization successfully averted a hyperinflationary spiral and catalyzed the beginnings of an economic recovery—with real GDP projected by multilateral institutions to rebound by roughly 4.5% to 5.5% in 2025 after an estimated 3.8% contraction in 2024—the underlying socioeconomic and external equilibria remained highly precarious.8 Net international reserves remained critically low despite a successful capital amnesty program, the vital agricultural export sector faced mounting competitiveness pressures due to relentless real exchange rate appreciation, and the International Monetary Fund (IMF) explicitly underscored the urgent, structural need to transition toward a flexible exchange rate regime and systematically dismantle the existing capital controls (the *cepo*) to ensure long-term sovereign debt sustainability.5

## **Simulation Architecture and Epistemological Boundaries**

The curation of seed data for the MiroFish multi-agent simulation engine requires an absolute adherence to chronological isolation. Because the pilot case seeks to evaluate the efficacy of a human rubric for qualitative and interpretable forecasting regarding Argentina's political-economic evolution in 2025, the model must not be contaminated by empirical truths that materialized after 31 January 2025\. Introducing post-cutoff data—such as the actual inflation rates for January 2025 published in mid-February, or the shifting market expectations surveyed after the implementation of the 1% crawling peg—would introduce fatal look-ahead bias, fundamentally invalidating the predictive scoring mechanism of the pilot.  
Consequently, the parametric initialization of the simulation must rely exclusively on macroeconomic, monetary, fiscal, and institutional variables that were verifiably in the public domain on or before the cutoff date. The curation architecture isolates primary institutional sources, specifically the Central Bank of the Argentine Republic (BCRA), the National Institute of Statistics and Censuses (INDEC), the Ministry of Economy (MECON), and apex multilateral organizations including the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the World Bank (WB), the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), and the Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC).3  
To simulate the psychological and behavioral baselines of economic actors, the data corpus integrates consensus forecasts from the private sector, predominantly BBVA Research and J.P. Morgan, alongside the BCRA's Market Expectations Survey (REM) collected at the end of December 2024\.8 By analyzing the transmission mechanisms of the Argentine economy—specifically the velocity of the crawling peg, the volume of Central Bank remunerated liabilities, the elasticity of fiscal revenues in a recovering economy, and the political economy of maintaining austerity amidst elevated poverty metrics—the MiroFish engine can accurately weight the utility functions and behavioral responses of distinct agents. These simulated entities—representing the domestic agricultural exporter, the international bondholder, the informal currency trader, the public sector union, and the central bank governor—must interact within the strict, bounded reality of early 2025\.

## **The Fiscal Anchor and the Mechanics of Structural Adjustment**

The fundamental cornerstone of the Milei administration's macroeconomic stabilization program was the establishment and rigid defense of a non-negotiable fiscal anchor. On 17 January 2025, the Ministry of Economy, led by Luis Caputo, officially announced the closing fiscal metrics for the 2024 calendar year, providing a critical validation of the government's austerity framework.2  
The National Public Sector (SPN) achieved a primary surplus of 1.8% of GDP and a financial surplus of 0.3% of GDP.3 This outcome represents one of the most violent and rapid fiscal consolidations in modern economic history, successfully correcting a massive financial deficit of 4.4% of GDP (or 4.6% excluding extraordinary 5G licensing revenues) inherited from the previous administration at the end of 2023\.1 For the simulation engine to accurately model the "Executive Authority" agent, it is essential to encode the precise mechanics of how this surplus was generated, as it dictates the political and economic friction the agent will face moving forward.

### **Decomposition of the 2024 Fiscal Contraction**

The fiscal surplus was not achieved through a systemic, structural modernization of the tax code or a broad-based expansion of organic tax revenues generated by economic growth. Rather, it was executed through aggressive, discretionary, and often asymmetric cuts to public expenditure—a strategy colloquially branded as the *motosierra* (chainsaw) approach. According to independent analysis of the Treasury's data, 15 of the 16 primary national expenditure categories experienced severe declines in real terms during 2024, resulting in an aggregate drop in primary spending of 27.5% when adjusted for inflation.16  
The depth of the cuts varied significantly across sectors, revealing the government's prioritization matrix:

* **Capital Transfers to Provinces:** Collapsed by 96% in real terms, effectively halting federally funded public works and infrastructure projects across the nation.16  
* **Real Direct Investment:** Contracted by 70%, further reflecting the freeze on capital expenditure.16  
* **Current Transfers to Provinces:** Dropped by 67.8%, placing immense fiscal strain on subnational governments.16  
* **Energy and Transport Subsidies:** Despite massive nominal increases in utility tariffs for end-users, the real-term expenditure on economic subsidies plummeted as the government shifted the cost burden to the private sector.16

The only category that evaded the austerity measures was the universal child allowance (Asignación Universal por Hijo \- AUH) and related social protection programs, which saw a real-term increase of 39%, reflecting the administration's targeted attempt to shield the absolute most vulnerable demographics from the brunt of the shock therapy.16

### **Simulation Implications: The Limits of Austerity**

For the MiroFish simulation, encoding the 2024 fiscal triumph must be immediately counterbalanced by the political and structural limits of its sustainability into 2025\. The IMF's Ex-Post Evaluation, released in early January 2025, praised the fiscal adjustment but issued a stark warning regarding its social toll. The report highlighted that the sharp correction resulted in poverty rates exceeding 50% during the first half of 2024\.11 The IMF explicitly noted that further strengthening of social support systems would be unconditionally necessary to sustain the public backing required for the ongoing, multi-year economic transformation.11  
Furthermore, BBVA Research's December 2024 macroeconomic outlook explicitly warned that looking ahead into 2025, "the scope for further expenditure cuts is significantly narrowing following the sharp adjustments implemented in 2024".18 Therefore, any simulated agent representing the fiscal authority will face extreme difficulty in maintaining the 1.7% primary surplus target for 2025 if the broader economic activity does not recover sufficiently to boost tax revenues organically.8  
In the simulation environment, "Provincial Governor" agents and "Public Sector Union" agents must be programmed with highly elevated friction coefficients. Having absorbed 96% and 67.8% cuts to their respective funding streams, provincial agents are mathematically cornered. If the simulated federal government refuses to restore funding, the model must allow for emergent behaviors, such as provinces issuing their own quasi-currencies (provincial bonds used as legal tender) or organizing coordinated legislative blockades against the executive's reform agenda.

## **Monetary Policy, Peso Liquidity, and the Central Bank Balance Sheet**

Simultaneous with the fiscal adjustment, the architecture of the Central Bank’s (BCRA) balance sheet underwent a radical transformation throughout 2024, establishing a new paradigm for monetary policy that must be accurately parameterized in the simulation's initial state as of January 2025\.

### **The Eradication of the Quasi-Fiscal Deficit**

A primary, existential objective of the incoming administration in late 2023 was to eliminate the BCRA's quasi-fiscal deficit. This deficit, driven by the exponential compounding of interest on the Central Bank's remunerated liabilities (Leliqs and later Pases), had reached a staggering 10.5% of GDP in 2023, representing a constant, endogenous engine of future money creation and inflation.11  
To neutralize this threat, the Ministry of Economy and the BCRA executed a systemic migration of these remunerated liabilities directly to the National Treasury. The final phase of this process involved the elimination of the Letras Fiscales de Liquidez (LEFI). By the end of 2024, the BCRA's expansion of the monetary base was no longer driven by the need to pay interest on its own debt, nor was it driven by direct monetary financing of the Treasury (which had been reduced to zero). Instead, the primary factor for the expansion of the monetary base was the BCRA's purchase of foreign currency in the official market and the gradual, controlled unwinding of the remaining LEFI holdings by financial entities.19  
The Central Bank established a strict quantitative target to anchor expectations: it proposed limiting the total volume of pesos in the economy to the amount of the Broad Monetary Base (Base Monetaria Amplia—the sum of monetary base and remaining remunerated liabilities) as it stood on 30 April 2024, which equated to $47.7 trillion current pesos, or roughly 9.1% of GDP.20

### **Interest Rates and Private Credit Expansion**

The migration of sovereign debt from the Central Bank to the Treasury fundamentally altered the incentive structures for domestic financial institutions. With the elimination of high-yielding, risk-free central bank paper that previously absorbed all excess banking liquidity, commercial banks were theoretically forced to redirect liquidity toward the private sector to generate returns.  
This structural shift began bearing fruit by the end of 2024\. The BCRA's December 2024 Monthly Monetary Report highlighted a definitive, incipient recovery in private sector credit in real terms, marking a reversal of a multi-year trend of financial disintermediation.19 However, the calibration of interest rates remained a highly contentious variable. Throughout much of 2024, the monetary policy rate was kept heavily negative in real terms to aid in the dilution of the central bank's liabilities. By 31 December 2024, the nominal annual monetary policy rate stood at 32%, with retail fixed-term deposits yielding roughly 33.95%.20  
The IMF's January 2025 Staff Report issued a prescriptive mandate regarding this dynamic: monetary policy must play a more meaningful and active role in economic stabilization. Specifically, the IMF instructed that authorities must ensure a positive real interest rate that appropriately responds to domestic inflation developments, which is deemed necessary to secure internal economic balance and incentivize domestic savings in local currency.11  
For the MiroFish simulation, the "Central Bank" agent faces a complex optimization problem. If it raises rates to satisfy the IMF and provide positive real returns to savers, it risks stalling the nascent expansion of private credit, thereby jeopardizing the V-shaped GDP recovery projected for 2025\. Conversely, if it keeps rates artificially low, it risks fueling demand for alternative dollar assets (such as the MEP or CCL parallel exchange rates), widening the exchange rate gap and triggering capital flight.

## **Exchange Rate Tensions: The Crawling Peg and the Cepo**

The most critical, volatile, and highly scrutinized variable for the simulation's short-term predictive capacity is the administration of the official exchange rate and the persistence of the *cepo* (foreign exchange controls).

### **The Transition from a 2% to 1% Crawling Peg**

Following the massive 120% step devaluation of the peso implemented immediately upon taking office in December 2023, the BCRA instituted a crawling peg regime, depreciating the official currency at a fixed, predetermined rate of 2% per month.5 Throughout 2024, this 2% crawl acted as a powerful, unyielding nominal anchor. Because it was consistently lower than the monthly inflation rate, it forced the prices of internationally traded goods (transables) to decelerate, successfully dragging headline inflation down from a peak of 25.5% in December 2023 to 2.7% by December 2024\.6  
However, by January 2025, the macroeconomic utility of the 2% crawl had inverted. Economic authorities, including BCRA Vice President Vladimir Werning, recognized that maintaining a 2% depreciation rate while domestic inflation converged toward that same 2% figure was actually preventing further disinflation. The crawl had transitioned from a stabilizing anchor into an inertial floor; instead of pulling prices down, it was replicating upward price hikes.5  
Consequently, following the confirmation of the 2.7% December inflation print, the government officially announced in mid-January 2025 that the crawling peg would be reduced to 1% monthly, effective 1 February 2025\.5 This policy shift introduces a massive, structural tension into the simulation framework: the zero-sum conflict between domestic disinflation and external competitiveness.

### **The Plight of the Agro-Exporter**

A profound third-order insight derived from this policy shift is the acute, compounding pressure placed on the *transables* sector, specifically the agricultural exporters who generate the vast majority of Argentina's hard currency. Because domestic inflation consistently outpaced the 2% monthly devaluation throughout the entirety of 2024, the real exchange rate appreciated significantly, eroding the competitiveness gained during the December 2023 devaluation.5  
Lowering the crawl to 1% exacerbates this trend geometrically. For the simulation engine, agents representing the agricultural sector must be programmed with parameters reflecting a severe margin squeeze. They are caught in a vise between softening international commodity prices, potential weather-related yield shocks, and persistently high domestic operational and logistical costs measured in hard currency.5  
Within the simulation, if these agro-export agents behave rationally according to their profit-maximization utility functions, they may choose to hoard their grain in silo bags, delaying the liquidation of their export harvests in anticipation of an eventual discrete devaluation or the total unification of the exchange rate. If this emergent behavior occurs, the Central Bank agent will fail to accumulate the foreign reserves necessary to meet external sovereign debt obligations and intervene in the currency markets, triggering a systemic crisis.

### **The IMF Mandate and the Exit from the Cepo**

The ultimate, binding constraint on the simulated future of Argentina is the availability of net international reserves and the timeline for removing the *cepo*. Despite the successful accumulation of reserves in 2024—aided heavily by an extraordinarily successful tax amnesty (*blanqueo de capitales*) that brought billions of dollars into the domestic financial system and compressed the gap between the official and parallel exchange rates to below 10%—net reserves remained fragile.14  
The IMF's January 2025 assessment is unequivocal: Argentina must execute a "credible strategy to unwind FX controls" and transition toward greater exchange rate flexibility.11 The IMF argues that the *cepo* creates profound economic distortions, severely deters long-term foreign direct investment, and suppresses the repatriation of dividends, ultimately stifling organic economic growth. However, removing the *cepo* prematurely, before the Central Bank has amassed sufficient reserve firepower, carries the asymmetric, catastrophic risk of triggering a sudden devaluation spike. Such a spike would immediately pass through to domestic prices, reigniting inflation, devastating real wages, and destroying the administration's primary political achievement.5  
The government’s publicly stated strategy, articulated repeatedly throughout January 2025, was to remove the controls "layer by layer" in a gradualist approach, rather than a shock removal.22 For the multi-agent engine, this implies that the timeline for full exchange rate unification cannot be hardcoded; it must be completely endogenous to the system's reserve accumulation dynamics.

## **Strategic Implications for Simulation Modeling**

The macroeconomic seed data curated strictly up to 31 January 2025 provides the MiroFish engine with a highly constrained, explosive, and structurally complex environment. The simulation engine is not merely projecting linear mathematical trends; it must resolve competing qualitative incentives and structural bottlenecks through agent interaction.  
The primary, overarching conflict the simulation must navigate is the **Competitiveness vs. Disinflation Trade-off**. By hardcoding the policy shift to a 1% crawling peg into the central bank agent 5, the engine will naturally suppress the price evolution of tradable goods, mathematically pushing simulated inflation downward toward the REM's 25.9% annual consensus.9 However, the simulation must accurately penalize the agricultural exporter agents for this policy. As their domestic, dollar-denominated costs remain elevated and international commodity prices soften, these exporter agents are mathematically incentivized to withhold the liquidation of their crops. This rational economic action directly drains the central bank agent's ability to accumulate reserves, creating a systemic balance-of-payments bottleneck.  
Simultaneously, the **Fiscal Constraint vs. Political Economy** conflict dominates the domestic sphere of the simulation. The executive agent enters the model run with immense financial credibility, having achieved a 0.3% financial surplus.4 Yet, the seed data proves unequivocally that this was achieved via a massive 27.5% real cut to expenditures.16 With baseline poverty established at above 50% 11, the simulated social agents (unions, informal workers, provincial governments) will exhibit high baseline volatility. If the simulated economic recovery—the 4.5% to 5.5% GDP growth forecasted by consensus 8—fails to generate organic tax revenues rapidly enough in the first simulated quarters, the executive agent will be forced into a binary, crisis-inducing choice: either abandon the fiscal anchor, which would trigger an immediate sovereign risk spike and bond sell-off, or deepen austerity beyond the mathematical limits of social tolerance, jeopardizing the stability required for the 2025 midterm elections.  
Lastly, the **Cepo Dilemma** functions as the master switch of the simulation's financial architecture. The IMF agent's utility function demands the removal of these capital controls to disburse further funds.11 If the executive agent lifts the *cepo* too early within the simulation timeline—prior to the simulated Central Bank amassing sufficient net reserves from the reluctant agro-exporters—the engine must trigger an immediate currency devaluation. This devaluation would violently invalidate the optimistic 35% inflation forecasts, reset the entire stabilization timeline, and trigger a deep recession.  
By bounding the simulation strictly to the knowledge available on 31 January 2025, the multi-agent engine is forced to independently navigate these exact, historically accurate structural tensions, providing a pristine environment for testing the qualitative forecasting rubric.
