# Bolivia election context: crisis and fragmented race

Source URL: https://www.as-coa.org/articles/poll-tracker-bolivias-2025-presidential-election

Published at: 2025-08-16

Source type: policy analysis / poll tracker

Bolivia entered the August 17, 2025 presidential election in a context of economic stress, political fragmentation and weakening confidence in the governing Movement Toward Socialism (MAS). The election came shortly after Bolivia's bicentennial and would choose a president for a five-year term. Polling available before the vote showed a dispersed field: no candidate was clearly near the threshold needed to win in the first round, and undecided voters were a large part of the electorate.

The economic backdrop was unusually important. Bolivia was facing high inflation, shortages of basic goods, fuel scarcity and pressure on foreign-currency availability. The economic model associated with the MAS governments had lost much of its earlier legitimacy. MAS had governed Bolivia since 2005, except for the 2019-2020 interim period, but by 2025 the party was weakened by the conflict between President Luis Arce and former president Evo Morales. That internal split made the governing camp less cohesive and complicated the left-wing electoral offer.

The constitutional runoff rule shaped expectations. A candidate could avoid a second round only by winning more than 50% of the vote, or by reaching 40% while leading the nearest competitor by at least 10 percentage points. Given the fragmented polling environment, a runoff was widely plausible before election day. The central uncertainty was not only whether there would be a second round, but which candidates would reach it.

The opposition field was also divided. Samuel Doria Medina, Jorge "Tuto" Quiroga and Manfred Reyes Villa were among the more visible opposition figures. Doria Medina was a businessman and former planning minister; Quiroga was a former president with a conservative and pro-market profile; Reyes Villa was the mayor of Cochabamba and campaigned on a right-leaning law-and-order platform. The left or MAS-linked side included Eduardo del Castillo, a former minister under Arce, and Andronico Rodriguez, then president of the Senate and a figure associated with the broader MAS political universe.

The campaign therefore combined several tensions at once: economic dissatisfaction, fragmentation inside MAS, competition among multiple opposition figures, and a high share of voters not firmly attached to any candidate. The pre-first-round picture was not a simple two-candidate race. It was a multi-candidate contest with a likely runoff and unclear finalist composition.

Rodrigo Paz was part of the broader opposition field but was not the dominant figure in many pre-election accounts. The visible race was often discussed around the better-known opposition names, the MAS split, and the economic crisis. The information available before the first round pointed to voter dissatisfaction and a possible anti-MAS shift, but did not by itself settle which opposition candidacy would become the strongest vehicle for that shift.

# Pre-first-round polling context

Source URLs:

- https://www.atlasintel.org/poll/bolivia-national-2025-08-15
- https://larazon.bo/nacional/2025/08/08/encuesta-tuto-quiroga-desplaza-a-samuel-doria-medina-del-primer-lugar/
- https://larazon.bo/nacional/2025/08/10/a-dias-de-las-elecciones-samuel-lidera-encuesta-con-216/

Published at: 2025-08-15

Source type: polling context

The final polling environment before Bolivia's August 17, 2025 first round remained fragmented and unstable. AtlasIntel published a national survey dated August 15, with fieldwork conducted from August 11 to August 14. The survey described itself as nationally representative and listed a sample size of 1,916 respondents with a margin of error of plus or minus 2 percentage points. It focused on voter preferences for the first round of the presidential election.

Local Bolivian coverage in early August also showed movement among the visible frontrunners. La Razon reported on August 8 that Jorge "Tuto" Quiroga had moved past Samuel Doria Medina into first place in one survey, nine days before the general election. That report presented Quiroga, Doria Medina and other opposition candidates as the central names in the race, with MAS-linked candidates weakened by party division and public dissatisfaction.

A second La Razon report, published August 10, described a Captura Consulting poll for Red Uno. In that survey, Samuel Doria Medina led with 21.6%, while Jorge Quiroga followed closely with 20.0%. The same report said the poll was conducted from July 27 to August 3 and included 2,500 interviews in urban, rural and Indigenous autonomous areas across Bolivia's nine departments, plus the city of El Alto.

These pre-election polls did not show a candidate near an outright first-round victory. The numbers clustered around the low twenties for the leading contenders, leaving space for late movement, undecided voters and polling error. The press coverage treated Doria Medina and Quiroga as major competitors, with different surveys showing different ordering at the top. The composition of a potential runoff was still uncertain.

The survey environment also reflected the difficulty of measuring a race shaped by protest voting, a fragmented governing party and changing voter alignments. Bolivia's opposition vote was not concentrated behind one figure before the first round. Several candidates claimed to represent change after MAS rule, and voters dissatisfied with the government still had multiple ways to express that preference.

Rodrigo Paz appeared in the race but was not consistently described as the principal frontrunner in the final pre-first-round coverage. The major polling question before election day was whether the visible opposition figures would consolidate support, whether left/MAS-linked candidates would retain enough votes to remain competitive, and how large the undecided or late-deciding electorate would be.

The available polling context therefore described a race with high uncertainty, close leading candidates, no first-round majority scenario and a likely second-round contest. It also established a baseline in which pre-election expectations were anchored more around Quiroga and Doria Medina than around a clear Paz-led race.

# Bolivia heads to presidential runoff

Source URL: https://apnews.com/article/bolivia-election-7d24bfd2bc87e4f312d574663aee9f5d

Published at: 2025-08-18

Source type: wire news

AP reported on August 18 that Bolivia was headed to an unprecedented presidential runoff after the August 17 first-round vote. Rodrigo Paz, a center-right lawmaker from a prominent political family, finished first and was set to face former president Jorge "Tuto" Quiroga in the October 19 runoff. The runoff would be the first presidential second round under Bolivia's current constitutional system.

The first-round result differed sharply from much of the pre-election polling narrative. Paz had lagged in polls before the vote, but AP reported him winning roughly 32% of votes cast. Quiroga received just over 26%. Candidates associated with the Movement Toward Socialism (MAS) performed very poorly, each below 10%, leaving the governing party outside the runoff.

Paz was described as a center-right senator and former mayor from Tarija, with a family name tied to Bolivian politics through his father, former president Jaime Paz Zamora. His ticket also included Edman Lara, a former police officer with an anti-corruption message and a direct style that drew attention during the campaign. Lara presented himself as new and different from professional politicians, and his message resonated with some voters cynical about corruption and the justice system.

The Paz-Lara campaign framed itself as a challenge not only to MAS rule but also to older political elites. AP quoted campaign language focused on rejecting "the same old people" from both left and right. The campaign's appeal included anti-corruption themes, frustration with political insiders, and dissatisfaction with the economic situation.

The report also placed the result in a broader context of economic pain. Bolivia faced inflation, fuel scarcity and shortages of dollars needed for imports. Voters who had once benefited from MAS-era prosperity were now confronting a weaker economy and a governing party divided by internal conflict. In that environment, the first round produced a runoff between two candidates outside MAS.

The result changed the structure of the election. Instead of a runoff between the best-known opposition figures from the pre-election polls, the second round would be between Paz and Quiroga. The first round also showed that some voter preferences had not been fully captured by pre-election expectations.

# MAS defeat and runoff realignment

Source URLs:

- https://www.investing.com/news/world-news/explainerwho-are-bolivias-presidential-runoff-contenders-promising-economic-overhaul-4197574
- https://www.reuters.com/world/americas/official-bolivia-presidential-count-shows-centrist-rodrigo-paz-surprise-lead-2025-08-17/
- https://apnews.com/article/bolivia-election-7d24bfd2bc87e4f312d574663aee9f5d

Published at: 2025-08-18

Source type: wire news / runoff explainer

Reuters described the runoff as a significant political shift after the worst electoral defeat for MAS in two decades. The first round created a second-round contest between centrist senator Rodrigo Paz and conservative former president Jorge "Tuto" Quiroga. With no candidate securing a first-round victory, the October 19 runoff would depend in part on which finalist could win over supporters of eliminated rivals.

AP similarly framed the vote as the end of an era for MAS, which had governed Bolivia almost uninterrupted since Evo Morales came to power in the mid-2000s. The ruling party's poor result left no MAS presidential candidate in the second round. Voters who had previously supported MAS, left-wing alternatives, protest options or other opposition candidates became central to the next phase of the race.

The first-round results showed Paz in first place, Quiroga in second, and Samuel Doria Medina in third. Doria Medina later endorsed Paz. That endorsement mattered because Doria Medina had been one of the prominent opposition figures before the first round and had drawn support from voters seeking economic change and a break from MAS rule.

The social geography of the vote was also important. AP reported frustration in places associated with MAS strength, including El Alto. Economic crisis, inflation, fuel scarcity and disillusionment with the governing party weakened old loyalties. Some voters in historically pro-MAS areas expressed fatigue with the party and anger over corruption, economic decline and the erosion of public trust.

Paz's running mate Edman Lara was part of that story. Lara's anti-corruption message and outsider style appealed to voters who felt that the justice system and political class were compromised. His presence gave the Paz ticket a different profile from older establishment opposition figures.

Quiroga, by contrast, entered the runoff as a former president and a better-known conservative figure with a pro-market orientation. His campaign emphasized change after MAS rule and a more forceful break with the outgoing political-economic model. The runoff therefore brought together two anti-MAS options with different styles, biographies and potential voter coalitions.

The campaign after August 18 was no longer only about punishing MAS. It was also about which non-MAS candidate could assemble a majority from a fragmented electorate: first-round Paz voters, Quiroga voters, Doria Medina supporters, other opposition voters, former MAS voters and citizens who had abstained or cast protest votes.

# Runoff candidate comparison

Source URL: https://www.as-coa.org/articles/bolivias-2025-presidential-runoff-comparing-candidates

Published at: 2025-10-08

Source type: policy analysis / candidate comparison

AS/COA compared Rodrigo Paz and Jorge "Tuto" Quiroga during the runoff campaign. Both candidates represented a break from MAS rule, but they offered different profiles to voters facing economic crisis, shortages and uncertainty over future reforms.

Quiroga was presented as a conservative former president with a pro-market reform identity. He briefly served as president from 2001 to 2002 and had previously worked in business and consulting. In the 2025 campaign, he framed his candidacy around ending what he described as two decades of failed socialist rule. His proposals included trade liberalization, fiscal adjustment, reforms to agriculture and hydrocarbons, and changes to state-owned companies. He also discussed reducing fuel subsidies and seeking support from institutions such as the International Monetary Fund and other lenders to replenish reserves and address shortages.

Quiroga also had a distinct foreign-policy profile. He criticized left-wing governments in the region, supported closer ties with the United States, and proposed breaking or changing relations with some governments aligned with the outgoing MAS foreign-policy approach. On lithium, he favored finding new investors and developing Bolivia's resources with a more open, investment-oriented strategy.

Paz was presented as a more moderate reform candidate. He campaigned under the slogan "Capitalism for All" and emphasized decentralization, productive credit and gradual economic modernization. His "50-50" proposal called for the central government to manage only half of public funds, with the other half going directly to regional governments. He also proposed selling loss-making state enterprises to Bolivian companies and using technology, including blockchain, to increase transparency and fight corruption.

On macroeconomic stabilization, Paz rejected using the IMF as the central answer, though he did not rule out other multilateral lenders. He favored renegotiating debt and pursuing a less abrupt adjustment path. He also expressed support for some social-assistance measures, including pension increases and income support for stay-at-home mothers. On foreign policy, Paz also signaled interest in improving relations with the United States, but without adopting exactly the same agenda as Quiroga.

The comparison also highlighted the role of the vice-presidential candidates. Paz's running mate Edman Lara brought an anti-corruption and outsider message that had gained traction in the first round. Quiroga's running mate Juan Pablo Velasco brought a younger technology and business profile but faced controversy over past social media posts.

The article presented the runoff as a choice between two candidates to the right of MAS, but not identical candidates. Both offered change, both spoke to the economic crisis, and both sought to move Bolivia away from MAS rule. Their differences lay in style, pace of reform, relationship to austerity, foreign policy, and the kinds of voter coalitions they could attract.

# Late runoff poll

Source URL: https://www.bolivia.com/actualidad/nacionales/tuto-quiroga-amplia-su-ventaja-sobre-rodrigo-paz-en-la-ultima-encuesta-electoral-543171

Published at: 2025-10-13

Source type: polling

A late runoff poll reported Jorge "Tuto" Quiroga ahead of Rodrigo Paz, with Quiroga at 44.9% and Paz at 36.5%. The poll was published less than a week before the October 19 runoff.

The reported gap was 8.4 percentage points. The poll appeared during the final stretch of the campaign, after the first-round surprise and after weeks of runoff campaigning.

The Bolivia.com article presented the survey as the latest electoral poll before the runoff. Its headline emphasized that Quiroga had expanded his advantage over Paz. The poll numbers placed Quiroga in first position and Paz in second, with a meaningful but not overwhelming difference between them.

The timing of the poll made it salient. It appeared after the first round had already narrowed the race to two candidates and after voters had several weeks to observe the candidates' second-round messages. The survey therefore reflected a later campaign environment than the fragmented first-round polls from August.

The article did not report a completed election result. It described voter intention before the October 19 vote. Its main quantitative information was the 44.9% figure for Quiroga, the 36.5% figure for Paz, and the 8.4-point gap between them.

# US relations before the runoff

Source URL: https://www.state.gov/secretary-rubio-and-deputy-secretary-landau-host-meeting-with-bolivian-presidential-candidates/

Published at: 2025-10-15

Source type: official statement

US officials met with Bolivian presidential candidates before the runoff and emphasized the possibility of improved relations with Bolivia after years of tension.

The statement concerned meetings with Bolivian presidential candidates and the possibility of a reset in bilateral ties. The issue appeared in the broader context of a post-MAS foreign policy shift, with both finalists potentially changing Bolivia's relationship with the United States.

The US State Department statement described engagement by senior US officials with Bolivian presidential candidates in the days before the runoff. The public framing focused on democratic engagement, bilateral relations and the possibility of cooperation after a period in which Bolivia's relationship with the United States had been strained.

The statement did not present polling numbers and did not identify a preferred winner. Its relevance was diplomatic and institutional: it showed that foreign relations were part of the campaign environment, and that a change in Bolivia's political direction could alter the country's external partnerships.

During the MAS era, Bolivia had often distanced itself from Washington and aligned more closely with left-leaning governments in the region. Both runoff finalists represented some degree of break from that foreign-policy pattern, although their styles and policy emphases differed. The official US statement appeared as one more piece of the late-campaign context surrounding economic stabilization, international financing and post-election diplomatic positioning.
